How many sets per muscle group per week do you need?

Gainfully · October 27, 2026 7 minute read

Most lifters make progress on roughly 10-20 hard sets per muscle group per week. Beginners can get by on 6-10, while experienced lifters often work in the upper half of that range. The number counts sets taken close to failure, not warm-ups, and it is a starting point you adjust based on how you recover and progress.

Below you get a simple definition of a "set", ranges by experience level, a four-day weekly plan with the sets added up per muscle group, and a fixed method for turning volume up or down.

What counts as a set?

Volume is the total number of sets you do for a muscle group in a week. To make numbers comparable, people count hard sets:

  • Close to failure. The set ends with 0-3 reps left in the tank. If you could have done 8 more reps, it is too easy to count in full.
  • Working sets, not warm-ups. Light warm-up sets do not count.
  • A reasonable rep range. Typically 5-30 reps with good technique.

Many exercises train more than one muscle. The bench press works your chest, but also your shoulders and triceps. A practical rule of thumb is to count sets where the muscle is the main mover in full and treat the indirect sets as a small bonus. If you want to be more precise, count indirect sets as half a set. That is a simplification, not a scientific law.

Sets per muscle group per week: ranges by experience

The table shows commonly used starting points. They are guardrails, not guarantees. Some people do better on more, others recover better on less.

Smaller muscles such as biceps, triceps, shoulders and calves get a lot of indirect work from the big lifts, so they often do well on fewer direct sets. Large muscles such as back and legs usually tolerate more.

If your main goal is maximal strength, you can do heavy sets with few reps without grinding to failure. About 10 sets per week for the lifts you want to improve is still a reasonable starting point.

Start low

Begin at the low end of your range and build up once you can see that you are recovering. It is easier to add sets than to recover from weeks of too much training.

Shoulders, biceps and triceps look low on direct sets, but they get work from the pressing and pulling movements. If you want to prioritize your arms, add 2-3 sets per muscle, not 10. The plan also spreads every muscle group over two sessions a week.

Should you spread sets over several days?

Yes, in practice it usually gives better quality. If you do 12 sets for your back, it is typically better to do 6 twice a week than 12 in one day. The last sets in a long session tend to get worse because you are tired, and it is hard to keep technique and effort up.

A good starting point is to train each muscle group twice a week and keep it to roughly 3-8 hard sets per muscle group per session. That fits well with a beginner plan that builds volume over time. The total weekly amount matters most, but splitting it makes it easier to recover and train hard each time.

Sets per muscle group are not the same as sets per exercise

People often mix the two up. "3-4 sets per exercise" tells you how many sets to do in one movement. Sets per muscle group is the sum across every exercise that hits that muscle. Three back exercises of 4 sets each is 12 sets, not 4. That is why a session with nine exercises quickly becomes too much, even if each exercise only has a few sets.

How to adjust your volume step by step

  1. Start at the low end. If you are experienced, begin with 8-10 sets per muscle group per week.
  2. Hold it for 3-4 weeks. Log weight, reps and sets, and check whether your numbers improve.
  3. If you progress and recover well, keep going. There is no reason to change something that works.
  4. If you stall but recover fine, add 1-2 sets per muscle per week. Change one thing at a time.
  5. If your numbers drop or you are constantly tired, cut back. Remove 2-4 sets or take a deload week.

Watch for signs you are doing too much: strength falling over several weeks, poor sleep, soreness that does not go away and no desire to train. If you have joint pain or pain that lingers, have a doctor or physiotherapist look at it instead of training through it. The most common mistakes with sets and volume

  • You count every set. If you count easy sets and warm-ups, the number looks higher than it is and you think you already train a lot. Count only sets close to failure.
  • You start at the top of the range. 20 sets per muscle group from day one is a quick route to fatigue. Build up over weeks.
  • You put everything on one day. Spread sets over two sessions a week so each session stays high quality.
  • You forget indirect sets. If you do a lot of pressing, your triceps and shoulders already get a large share. Do not add even more without a reason.
  • You change the numbers every week. Volume takes time to judge. Give a change 3-4 weeks before you decide on it.
  • You do not keep records. Without notes you cannot tell whether more sets actually give you more reps or more weight on the bar.

If you only train three days a week, a full-body plan can still give you two sessions per muscle group. Pick a few big exercises per session and make sure you reach your weekly target without making each session longer.

Frequently asked questions

How many sets per muscle group per week should a beginner do?

Most beginners progress on 6-10 hard sets per muscle group per week, spread over 2 sessions. Start at the low end, learn the technique and build up slowly as you recover well.

Is 20 sets per muscle group per week too much?

For many people it is at the high end. Some experienced lifters can handle 15-20 sets for a priority muscle for a period, but only if sleep, food and recovery are in order. If your numbers drop or you are constantly tired, cut back.

Do warm-up sets count?

No. You count working sets taken close to failure. Light warm-up sets give too little stimulus to count.

How often should I train a muscle group per week?

Twice a week is a good starting point, because it makes it easier to spread sets and recover. The total weekly amount matters most.

Can I progress on fewer sets if I train hard?

Yes, especially as a beginner. Fewer sets close to failure can beat many easy sets. But do not go below roughly 4-6 sets if your goal is to build muscle.
