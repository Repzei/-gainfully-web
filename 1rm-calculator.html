What is a 1RM?

1RM (one rep max) is the maximum weight you can lift for exactly one complete repetition with correct form. It is the gold standard for measuring absolute strength in the gym.

Your 1RM is the foundation of all percentage-based strength programs. Whether you follow a 5/3/1, linear progression, or a percentage-based powerlifting program - everything is calculated from your 1RM.

Deadlift 1RM

For most lifters the deadlift is the lift where they move the most weight. Many pull around 1.1-1.3 times their squat 1RM. Long arms help, and grip often gives out before the legs and back do.

A max deadlift loads the lower back the most when technique slips. Keep the bar close, brace your core and pull your shoulders down before the bar leaves the floor, and stop the attempt if your lower back rounds. Never try a new max on the last set of a hard session.

Use a set of 3-5 clean reps in the calculator. In long deadlift sets, grip and breathing start to limit you, which makes the estimate less precise. Calculate your deadlift 1RM

Bench press 1RM

The bench press is the lightest of the three big lifts. For many lifters it lands at 60-75% of their squat 1RM. Grip width and how much you arch change how far the bar travels, so only compare numbers done with the same technique.

Never attempt a max bench alone without a spotter or safety arms. Keep your feet on the floor, your shoulder blades pulled together and your thumbs wrapped around the bar. A thumbless grip is a classic reason the bar slips.

A set of 3-8 reps gives a good estimate, as long as the bar touches your chest on every rep. Calculate your bench press 1RM

Squat 1RM

For most lifters the squat sits between the bench press and the deadlift. Depth matters: strength numbers usually assume a squat to at least parallel, with the hip crease level with the knees. A half squat gives a higher number that does not compare.

Take max attempts in a rack with the safety bars set just below the bottom of your squat. Warm up with rising weights, for example 50%, 70% and 85% of your expected max, and keep the same depth as in your working sets.

Use a set of 3-6 reps in the calculator. Long squat sets quickly turn into a conditioning test, which makes the estimate less precise. Calculate your squat 1RM

Relative strength: your 1RM compared to bodyweight

Relative strength is your 1RM divided by your bodyweight. If you deadlift 120 kg and weigh 80 kg, your relative strength is 1.5 × bodyweight. It lets you compare lifters of different sizes and see progress even when your own weight changes. Enter your bodyweight in the calculator and it does the math for you.

The three formulas

Epley (1985)
1RM = weight x (1 + reps / 30)
Brzycki (1993)
1RM = weight x (36 / (37 - reps))
Landers (1985)
1RM = weight / (1.013 - 0.0267123 x reps)

The average of all three is the most reliable estimate. For 2-6 reps all three formulas converge to nearly the same result. Above 10 reps they diverge, so stick to 2-8 reps for the best estimates.

How to use your 1RM in training

Strength development

Work at 85-95% of your 1RM for 1-5 reps. This range develops maximum strength and improves the neural adaptations that make you stronger.

Hypertrophy (muscle growth)

Work at 67-85% of your 1RM for 6-12 reps. This range maximizes muscle growth while still building strength.

Muscular endurance

Work at 50-67% of your 1RM for 15+ reps. Lower intensity, higher volume - builds work capacity.

Frequently asked questions

Which formula is most accurate?

Epley is most widely used and accurate for general use. Brzycki is slightly better for low reps (1-6). Landers performs well for moderate reps (5-10). The average of all three is the safest bet.

Is it safe to test my actual 1RM?

Yes, with proper technique and warm-up. But for most lifters, estimating from submaximal weights (3-8 reps) is safer and reduces injury risk - especially for the squat and deadlift.

How often should I test my 1RM?

Your estimated 1RM updates every time you log a set in Gainfully. An actual max test is typically done every 8-16 weeks, at the end of a training block.
