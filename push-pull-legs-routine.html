Push Pull Legs Routine: The Complete Split with Exercises, Sets and Reps

Gainfully · October 20, 2026 7 minute read

Push pull legs, often shortened to PPL, splits your training into three kinds of sessions: push, pull and legs. It suits most people who train three to six days a week, because you can dial the number of days up or down without changing the basic idea. Below is a complete routine with exercises, sets and reps.

This guide shows which version fits your schedule, how to progress, and which mistakes most often stall your results.

What is a push pull legs split?

The idea is to group muscles that work together in the same movements, so you never train the same muscle two days in a row. The split looks like this:

  • Push: chest, shoulders and triceps. Exercises like bench press, overhead press and triceps work.
  • Pull: back, biceps and rear delts. Exercises like rows, pulldowns, pull-ups and curls.
  • Legs: quads, hamstrings, glutes and calves. Exercises like squats, Romanian deadlifts, leg press and calf raises.

Because each session has its own muscle groups, you can train hard and still give the muscles you just worked time to recover while you train the others. That is why the split is popular with beginners and experienced lifters alike.

How many days a week should you train PPL?

It depends on your time and your recovery. The more days you train, the more often you hit each muscle group, but the more you also need to sleep and eat. Here are the four most common versions:

Train six days a week and you get the highest frequency: two sessions per muscle group. Train three days and you get one. Both work, and most people do best on the version they can actually keep up for months. If you only have three days, you can add one set per exercise to keep weekly volume up.

Start with three days

If PPL is new to you, or you are coming back after a break, start with three days a week for four to six weeks. Put a rest day between sessions, and only add a fourth day when you recover well between workouts.

The routine has two versions of each session, A and B, so you vary the exercises and hit the muscles from different angles. Warm up with a few light sets on the first heavy exercise, but not on every exercise. Rest about 2-3 minutes after heavy lifts like bench press and squat, and 1-2 minutes after the rest. If the routine is too much, drop one set per exercise. If it feels too easy and you recover well, add one set to the exercises where you want to improve most.

The routine is built for a regular gym with barbells, dumbbells, cables and machines. If a piece of equipment is missing, swap in a similar exercise for the same muscle, for example dumbbell press instead of bench press.

How to pick weights and add load

A routine only works if the load goes up over time. A simple method is double progression: each exercise has a rep range, for example 6-8. You start with a weight where you can hit the bottom of the range on every set. When you can hit the top of the range on every set, you add weight and start at the bottom again.

  • How hard? End most sets with 1-3 reps in reserve. You do not need to go to failure on every set.
  • How much more? Typically 2.5 kg (5 lb) on upper body lifts and 5 kg (10 lb) on leg lifts, or less if you have small plates.
  • What if you stall? Give it 3-4 weeks before judging. See the plateau guide for a checklist and deload.

If you want to know how heavy your working weights should be relative to your max, use the 1RM calculator.

PPL, upper/lower or full body?

PPL is not the only good split. If you only train two or three days a week, a full-body routine often makes more sense, because you hit each muscle several times a week with few sessions. With four days, upper/lower is a good choice where each muscle gets two sessions. PPL comes into its own at five to six days, where you can spread volume over more sessions and keep each one shorter, typically 45-70 minutes. Choose the split you can stick to, not the one that sounds most advanced.

Common PPL mistakes

  • You skip legs. Leg day is the first to drop off, and you end up with a strong upper body and weak legs. Schedule legs when you have the most energy.
  • You train six days without recovering. If you are always tired, your lifts drop or you sleep badly, go down to four or five days.
  • You keep changing exercises. Without the same exercise for several weeks you cannot tell whether you are getting stronger.
  • You do too many sets. More is not always better. Start at the low end of the range and add sets only when you can recover.
  • You do not track the numbers. Without notes you do not know whether you are actually adding weight.

If you have pain that lingers, or an injury, talk to a doctor or physiotherapist before you continue with the routine. Eating too little will also make progress hard. You can find your calorie needs with the TDEE calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Is push pull legs good for beginners?

Yes, in the 3-day version. You train your whole body across a week, get rest days between sessions and learn the main lifts. If you would rather train the whole body every session, a full-body routine is an equally good option.

How many days a week should I do push pull legs?

Three to six. Three days give each muscle group one hard session a week, six days give two. Pick the number of days you can keep up for months while still sleeping and recovering well.

In what order should I run push, pull and legs?

The order is not critical. A classic cycle is push, pull, legs, rest, then repeat. Put a rest day after legs if they leave you the most tired, and avoid stacking two heavy sessions for the same muscle group back to back.

How many sets per muscle group per week do I need?

A typical range is 10-20 sets per muscle group per week for most trained lifters. Beginners progress with fewer. The six-day routine here lands at about 9-14 sets per major muscle group, so you have room to add.
